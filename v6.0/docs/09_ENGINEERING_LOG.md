Completing the Original Hardware SetupI connected all the required components in the original Stage 4 design:* ESP32 DEVKIT_C* TB6612FNG motor driver* Left and right Hiwonder motors* 2 A automotive fuse and inline fuse holder* Self-locking emergency-stop button* Motor power supply* Control and power wiringAfter checking the connections, I connected the ESP32 to my computer.### 2. Uploading and Testing the ProgramThe motor-control program was uploaded to the ESP32 successfully. The serial monitor could receive my commands and return normal status messages, which showed that:* the ESP32 was working;* the program had been uploaded correctly;* the serial communication was functioning;* the command-processing section of the program was responding.I entered the following command:`LF 40 300`This command instructed the left motor to move forward at a speed value of 40 for a programmed duration value of 300.The ESP32 accepted the command and returned a normal completion message, but the left motor did not move.I then increased the speed value from 40 to 70 in case the original PWM value was too low to overcome the motor’s starting resistance. However, the motor still did not move.### 3. Troubleshooting the Original DesignI repeatedly checked the wiring and did not find any obvious missing or incorrectly connected wires. Additional tests also failed to produce motor movement.However, I did not have a multimeter, so I could not measure:* the voltage reaching the TB6612FNG;* whether the fuse and emergency-stop circuit had electrical continuity;* whether the ESP32 and motor driver shared a reliable ground;* whether the TB6612FNG was producing an output voltage;* whether the motor power supply could provide enough current.Because I was also unfamiliar with the TB6612FNG, emergency-stop switch and automotive-fuse circuit, I could not continue troubleshooting the system systematically.### 4. Researching an Alternative DesignI decided to pause the original TB6612FNG design and research another approach.While reading an Arduino electronics book, I learned about the L298N motor-driver module. A standard L298N contains two motor-control channels and can independently control the two DC motors used by Atlas.I therefore designed a revised Stage 4 setup using:* the existing ESP32;* the existing Hiwonder motors;* the existing 2 A fuse and emergency-stop button;* an L298N motor-driver module;* an adjustable DC bench power supply;* a digital multimeter;* suitable power wires and GPIO signal wires.### 5. Selecting the New Power SupplyI initially considered several relatively expensive power supplies. After comparing products on Taobao, I found that a less expensive adjustable supply could meet the requirements of the current test.I first considered a 1 A model, but its current capacity was too low for two motors. I then considered a 3 A model and finally selected a **3–12 V, 5 A adjustable DC power supply** to provide more current capacity for later Atlas experiments.This power supply will be used for desktop development and motor testing. Because it must be connected to a wall outlet, it cannot serve as the robot’s final mobile battery. The mobile power system will be selected later after the robot’s actual current requirements have been measured.## ResultThe original Stage 4 motor test was not completed successfully because the motors did not move. However, I verified that the ESP32 program and serial communication were functioning.I also identified the limitations of the original troubleshooting process and prepared a revised Stage 4 design based on the L298N, an adjustable 5 A bench power supply and proper diagnostic equipment.## Next StepAfter the new components arrive, I will:1. measure the power supply output with a multimeter;2. test one motor directly and briefly;3. connect and verify the L298N power circuit;4. test one motor through the L298N;5. test the second motor;6. test both motors together only after the individual tests succeed.
